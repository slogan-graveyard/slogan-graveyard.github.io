The Meritocracy Myth

Conflating market luck with personal labor.

It is the ultimate shield of the wealthy: the claim that their fortune is the direct result of their individual effort. When a millionaire says, “I worked hard for this,” they are telling a half-truth. No one "works" their way to a million-pound house by earning a salary; they get there by owning an asset during a boom.

The slogan relies on the deliberate blurring of earned income and capital appreciation. One comes from the sweat of your brow; the other comes from a favorable interest rate and the luck of the calendar. To claim the latter as a reward for the former is not just a fallacy—it is a moral distortion.

The housing boom was a systemic event, not a personal achievement. The individuals who bought cheap in the 70s and 80s didn't work harder than the people buying now; they simply bought in earlier. The reward for that timing—vast, unearned wealth—has been reframed as a sign of personal virtue, which conveniently justifies keeping that wealth untaxed and unshared.

By framing asset gains as "merit," the wealthy can argue that they deserve to keep every penny of their windfall, even when that windfall was generated by the very society that now provides their safety net.

Hard work may have gotten you into your house in 1975, but it didn't make your house worth a million pounds today. Stop crediting your ambition for a gift from the market.
